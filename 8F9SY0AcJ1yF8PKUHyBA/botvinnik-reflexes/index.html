Botvinnik System - Quick Reflexes

How to use this note: build the setup, find Black's first move in Part 1, follow the plan in Part 2, and keep Part 3's tactical reflexes running in every game. Each entry gives the reflex in bold first, then why, then the game it came from. The table at the bottom is the thirty-second version.

The One Move-Order Rule: Fianchetto Before e4

Move order matters: e4 comes AFTER g3 + Bg2. The fianchetto first, then the centre. That's the whole recipe.

Why: an early e4 hands Black a free developing hit. In the July 2026 session you played 3.e4 (before the bishop was home) in four White games, and in three of them Black got exactly that: ...Bb4 pinning (kvW4Vv0b, G5oZ2130) or ...Bc5 + ...Ng4 poking at f2 (QJ2Q0ZNc).

Part 1: The Opening Tree

Find Black's first move. In every branch the destination is the same setup; what changes is the one or two moves that get you there safely.

Against 1...d5

Reflex: 1.c4 d5 2.cxd5

Why: Just take. Top engine move (+0.61 at depth 20) and the most popular master move.

Common misconception: "Taking lets their queen come to d5" - but that's not how the main line goes. After 2.cxd5, Black usually plays 2...c6, 2...Nf6 or 2...e6, not 2...Qxd5.

Why NOT 2.Nc3: After 2.Nc3, Black has 2...d4! and your knight has no good square — the engine's best is the humiliating retreat 3.Nb1 (eval -0.90 for White).

The Reversed Dragon: 1.c4 e5 2.Nc3 Nf6 3.g3 d5

Reflex: 4.cxd5 — the same "resolve the tension yourself" medicine as the ...c6/...d5 counter in Part 2.

This is not a sideline. In sub-1400 bullet, ...d5 is Black's most popular answer to 3.g3 — 144,228 games, 19.8% of the position, ahead of ...Nc6, ...Bb4 and ...Bc5. Expect it roughly one game in five.

Why: 4.cxd5 is engine-best (+0.21) and the field's choice (59.7%). The natural-looking 4.Bg2 is -0.67 — it lets Black resolve the tension on their terms with ...dxc4, and now your c-pawn is gone and you have nothing for it.

Against 1...c6 (Caro-Kann by Rote)

Reflex: 1.c4 c6 2.Nc3 d5 3.cxd5 cxd5 4.d4. Pawn on d5, knight on c3: stop ...d4 before g3.

Why: this is the 1...d5 2.Nc3? d4! trap one move later. After the exchange on d5, Black's d-pawn can still advance to d4 and kick your knight, and 4.g3?! d4! 5.Nb1 is -0.72. Only about one sub-1400 player in ten finds ...d4, but you don't need to give them the chance. 4.d4 (+0.33, engine-best) shuts it out for good. Then develop with Bf4, e3, Nf3, Bd3, O-O. This is an Exchange-Slav structure, not the Botvinnik, so don't go looking for f4-f5.

Then watch d5. In loss D5io7BOs (Oct 2026, vs maia1) you played 4.g3 and the bot didn't punish it. The game was level until Black played a rote ...e5: 9.d4 e5 10.dxe5 Nxe5. Now d5 is isolated: no pawn on the c- or e-file can ever defend it. Three white pieces hit it (Nc3, Bg2 and the d1 queen down the open file); only two guard it (the f6 knight and the d8 queen).

Reflex: their ...e5 left d5 alone? Count attackers and take. 11.Nxd5! Nxd5 12.Qxd5 Qxd5 13.Bxd5 wins a clean pawn (+0.52). You played 11.e4? instead. That's the Botvinnik push in the wrong structure: it gave the d-pawn something to capture (-0.45) and opened the d-file toward your own d1. Two moves later 13.Rd1? walked into the old d-file wound (see "After a Queen Trade" in Part 3).

Against an Early ...f5 (Anglo-Dutch Setups)

Reflex: Keep building, and hit the centre with d4 instead of hurrying e4. After 1.c4 e5 2.Nc3 f5 the engine likes 3.e3/3.d4/3.g3 (~+0.4); your 3.e4 was playable (+0.29) but no better than the normal plan.

Why e4 is the wrong break here: they've already played ...f5, so e4 ...fxe4 opens their f-file, not yours — the exact file their rook castled onto. Your f4-f5 lever doesn't exist against a Dutch (their pawn is already on f5), so the d4 break is your plan B.

The games: two July games featured it: 1...f5 (Bgx5Fy4v) and 1.c4 e5 2.Nc3 f5 (aUuhSCrF). New territory, hence the reflex. In Bgx5Fy4v, 6.e4?! dropped the eval from +0.4 to -0.5 for exactly the open-f-file reason.

Bonus lesson from that game (11.e5??): a pawn that marches into their camp needs an escort. 11.e5 was attacked by a knight and defended by nothing (-1.65); the engine wanted 11.cxd5 first (+1.39). Count defenders before you push. (Same habit as counting e4's defenders in Part 3.)

Part 2: The Middlegame Plan

When you're set up: Pawns on c4, d3, e4. Pieces developed. King on h2.

The push: f4, then f5.

Kh2 Before f4 (It's About the h3 Pawn)

Reflex: tuck the king first — Kh2 personally guards h3 — and f4 comes with force (Kh2 is the engine's top move, +1.15).

Why (corrected): the old note said "so ...Qh4+ isn't a thing" — that was wrong, and you rightly went "huh": with a pawn on g3 a queen landing on h4 never gives check (the pawn blocks the h4–e1 diagonal, and after Kh2 the h3 pawn blocks the file) and simply hangs to gxh4. The real point is the h3 pawn. Club players love parking a queen + bishop battery on the c8–h3 diagonal (...Be6/...Bf5 + ...Qd7). If you play f4 with your king on g1, they win the h3 pawn with ...Bxh3! and your king cover unravels (engine: -0.63).

The d5 Knight Stays on d5: Don't Trade Your Best Piece for Their Worst

Reflex: before any trade, ask "whose piece is better?" If it's mine, keep it. A knight on d5 that no pawn can kick is usually the best piece on the board in this setup.

Why: a trade is a deal. Once your pawns are on c4, d3, e4 (and often g4), your g2 bishop stares at a wall of its own pawns, and Black's light-squared bishop on g6 or f7 bites the same wall from the other side. The knights are the pieces that matter. Swap your knight for their bishop and you're left with your shut-in bishop against their knight, which is paying full price for junk.

The game: loss vjcIygbx (Oct 2026, vs maia1). It was a level game after a clean setup (9.f3 against ...Bg4, straight out of Part 3). Here, with White to move, the knights on d5 and e5 are the two best pieces and the bishops on g2 and g6 the two worst. The engine's move was 19.g5 (+0.49, green), keeping the knight and taking space; quiet moves like 19.Rcd1 also hold. You played 19.Ne7+ (red), a check that just moved your best piece off its best square (-0.20), then 20.Nxg6+ fxg6 completed the bad trade and opened the f-file for Black (-1.21). The e5 knight spent the rest of the game eating d3 and c4.

Even after 19.Ne7+ Kh8 it wasn't too late: 20.Nd5, going back home, was still level (-0.20).

Part 3: Tactical Reflexes (Any Line)

These aren't tied to one opening branch. Run them every game.

The Bg4 Pin and the ...Nd4 Alarm

Reflex when ...Nd4 is in (or one move away): f3! Blocks the diagonal, kicks the bishop, and prepares f4 — engine-verified +0.53 in that exact position, while h3?? is -0.45:

Situation: You've played Nge2 and they pin with ...Bg4, eyeing your queen on d1.

First, don't panic — count e2. With your knight on c3, e2 is defended three times (Qd1, Nc3, Ke1). One bishop pinning is not a threat: if ...Bxe2 you recapture and shrug. In this typical picture the engine's top move is simply h3, asking the bishop the question (...Bxe2 Nxe2 and you're fine):

The real alarm bell is ...Nd4. Once a Black knight lands on d4 (or can jump there next move), everything changes: after you castle, ...Nxe2+ comes with check, and the pin means your queen recaptures into ...Bxe2. That's precisely how you lost your queen in the January game 4f4fSre5: 7...Nd4 8.h3?? Nxe2+! 9.Qxe2 Bxe2. The old version of this note garbled that into "h3 always loses the queen" — it doesn't; it needed the d4 knight.

Bullet shortcut: if you don't have time to count, f3 is the never-loses-the-queen move. It costs a sliver of eval when h3 was possible, but it's never a disaster.

Qa4+ Picks Up Loose Things

Reflex: while Black's king is still on e8 and something of theirs is loose on the queenside, Qa4+ is always worth a half-second look — especially before any automatic recapture. Check first, collect second.

Why: the check hits the king on the a4–e8 diagonal, and next move the queen scoops whatever is loose. From July loss G5oZ2130, twice in three moves the engine screamed the same move and it's a beautiful pattern. Black had grabbed on c4 (...Nxc4) and then sacked ...Bxg4. Don't auto-recapture — Qa4+! picks up the loose knight on c4 next move (+2.4, vs +0.9 for the recapture):

Broadened (Aug 2026), because this has now been missed twice. The trigger is not just "a loose piece" — a Black pawn sitting on c4 counts too. In jLCN0ZG3 the pawn on c4 was the loose thing, the king was on e8, and Qa4+ was both the engine's move and the field's most popular choice; instead the game continued 5.e4 and the pawn was simply gone (full line and numbers in the Reversed Dragon section of Part 1).

Whenever your c-pawn disappears onto c4 in Black's hands, the question is never "how do I finish my setup?" — it's "how do I get it back?" and the answer is usually a check on a4.

After a3+b4, ...a5 Attacks Your KNIGHT (Not Just Your Pawn)

Reflex: the moment ...a5 appears, move the knight — 13.Na4 (-0.80, engine's top move). It steps off the attacked square and hits the b6 bishop, so it's not a passive retreat.

Why ...a5 is not just a pawn poke: once your b-pawn has gone to b4, b2 is empty and your Nc3 has lost its pawn cover. When the a-pawns come off, the b4 pawn attacks c3 directly. A pawn attacking a knight means you lose a piece for a pawn — the exchange is never "fine."

And you can't just take back. After 13...axb4, the natural 14.axb4?? loses material to a pin most people don't see: your a3 pawn is pinned to an undefended rook on a1 by the rook on a8. The b4 pawn looks free; grabbing it drops the exchange.

The game: from jLCN0ZG3 (Aug 2026), the move that turned a manageable game into a lost one. You'd built the queenside with a3 and b4. Black played 12...a5, and you continued the setup with 13.Kh2 — the eval fell from -0.91 to -2.76, and two moves later the knight was gone. In the game 14.Bg5?? ignored the attack entirely and 14...bxc3 just won the piece. Once you're at move 14 the damage is done — the best on offer is the miserable 14.Nd1 (-3.03).

Reflex: a3+b4 is a commitment, not free space. It permanently removes the b2 defender from your Nc3, and it makes the a-file a liability for your own Ra1. When they hit b4, deal with the knight before any setup move.

Long-Diagonal Bishops: Scan Before You Land a Rook

Reflex: before placing a piece on a square, name what attacks it. Bishops that have sat on one diagonal for fifteen moves become furniture — that's exactly when they bite.

The game: jLCN0ZG3 again, move 25 — you had fought all the way back from -4.8 to +0.85 with a genuinely good attacking sequence, then played 25.Rd4?? and 25...Bxd4 simply took it (+0.85 → -4.21).

The bishop on b6 had been staring down the a7–g1 diagonal since move 9. Rd4 was chasing the queen on a2 and never looked at what already controlled the landing square.

The engine's move was 25.Qg5+ (+1.01) — check first, then bring the rook. Same "check first, collect second" idea as the Qa4+ pattern; it costs nothing and often gains everything.

Pieces Are Curtains: When One Moves, Look Behind It

Reflex: after every opponent move, ask "what did that piece just stop blocking?" It takes half a second: trace the line behind the square it left. Most common curtains in your games: - Nf6 in front of Qd8 → aims at h4/g5 once you storm - Nf6 in front of Bg7 (KID-style setups) → the long diagonal to c3, b2, a1 - Nd4 in front of Bc5 (vs 1...e5) → the a7–g1 diagonal at f2 and your king

The principle: a piece standing on a line hides whatever is behind it. When an opponent's piece moves away, especially when it retreats and looks harmless, a long-range piece behind it (queen, bishop, rook) can suddenly see your half of the board. Retreats are where this hides, because a backward move doesn't feel like a threat.

Why this matters in the Botvinnik storm: Black's queen on d8 points down the d8–h4 diagonal (e7, f6, g5, h4). In almost every game, Black's f6 knight is the curtain on that line. Push h4 (or g5) and you've put a pawn on that diagonal, where nothing but the knight separates it from the queen. From then on, every move of the f6 knight is a question about h4.
